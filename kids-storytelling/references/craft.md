# Story Craft — age bands, vocabulary, and scaffolds

Read this before writing any story. It pins the concrete numbers so you don't pitch too high or low.

## Vocabulary & length by age

| Age | Unique words | Sentence length | New words |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6–7 | ~400–800 | 5–10 words | Sight words, CVC patterns, blends & digraphs (ch, sh, th, bl, cr, st) |
| 8–9 | ~800–1,500 | 8–15 words | Complex vocabulary allowed **with context clues** |
| 9–11 | 1,500+ | 10–20 words | Domain and Tier 2 words, defined in context |

**Tier 2 words are the teaching targets** — useful across contexts ("curious," "hesitated," "clever") — not rare jargon. Introduce at most 2–3 new Tier 2 words per story for ages 6–8, 4–5 for 9–11, each defined by context on first use.

## Length by format

| Format | Length | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Picture-book prose | 400–1,000 words | 32-page standard; rhythm matters |
| Rhyming picture book | 500–700 words | See verse rules below |
| Early reader | 500–1,200 words | Short chapters or sections |
| Chapter-book excerpt | 800–1,500 words | One complete arc segment |
| Curriculum mentor text | 100–250 words | Subordinate to the lesson |

## Verse and rhyme rules (when rhyming is requested)

1. **Draft in prose first.** Then convert. Rhyme that drives the story produces forced inversions ("Said the cat happy").
2. **Story first, then rhyme.** If a rhyme fights the plot, the rhyme loses.
3. **Consistent meter.** Read it aloud; every line should survive being spoken. If you can't read it naturally, rewrite it.
4. **Perfect rhymes by default.** Near rhymes only when a perfect rhyme forces awkwardness.
5. **Never park a weak word at the rhyme position.** No rhyming "the" or "a".
6. Common schemes: AABB (youngest), ABAB, ABCB (older).

## Character craft

- **One clear protagonist** with a want the reader can name in a sentence.
- **Want vs. obstacle** is the engine — state the want early (first 20% of the story).
- Side characters get **one distinguishing trait each** — enough to tell them apart, no more.
- Character voice comes from **child-logic observations** ("The soup smelled like wet socks, but Grandma looked so happy that Lan ate it anyway").
- Names: diverse and setting-appropriate; easy to pronounce aloud for the age band. In Vietnamese stories, use familiar names (Mai, Nam, Hùng, Lan) unless a reason not to.

## Moral framing (show, don't tell)

- The lesson appears **once**, at the climax, as a **choice with a cost** — the character must give something up or risk something to do the right thing.
- Consequences flow naturally from actions; no narrator intervention.
- Trust the reader to extract the theme. The story ends on the character's changed behavior or the restored world — not on a stated moral.
- Themes that work at this band: kindness, courage, honesty, perseverance, self-acceptance, sharing, respect for nature.

## Curiosity hooks (openings that work)

- Start **in motion** — the character is already doing something slightly wrong or slightly wonderful.
- Start with a **sensory specific** — a smell, a sound — not weather or time-of-day.
- Ask a question the reader didn't know they had ("Nobody in the village knew why the river had stopped singing.")
- For curriculum passages: open with the character meeting the problem the lesson teaches.

## Endings

- **6–7:** restore the world; the refrain resolves; everyone safe.
- **8–9:** the character changed in one visible way.
- **9–11:** the change is shown through a choice or action in the final scene; resist explaining it.

## Common failure modes

| Failure | Fix |
|---|---|
| Stated moral ("The lesson is…") | Cut it; end one beat later on action |
| Adults solve the problem | Remove the adult or sideline them; the kid acts |
| Too many problems | Merge into one; the rest are scenery |
| Vocabulary over ceiling | Swap for Tier 2 or define in context |
| Rhyme distorts syntax | Back to prose, re-rhyme |
| Beautiful but plotless | Kill the darling; want → obstacle → climax |
| Teaching replaces story | Hide the lesson inside the climax choice |
